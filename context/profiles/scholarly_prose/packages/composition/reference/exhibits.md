# Figures and Tables

A figure or table is read out of order, skimmed before the surrounding prose, and often revisited alone. It therefore carries two obligations at once: it must be interpretable on its own terms, and it must be integrated with the argument the prose makes. An exhibit that satisfies neither — opaque in isolation, disconnected from the text — is a common and avoidable source of reader confusion.

## Captions Decode and Conclude

A caption is one to two sentences that decode the visual and state the takeaway. It is not a restatement of the title. Decoding means telling the reader what the axes, rows, columns, arrows, or symbols mean in plain words; concluding means stating what the exhibit shows for the argument.

Strong captions define their own notation in plain language: a results table whose caption explains, row by row, what each reported event denotes — "the event UB > true denotes the case where the true value falls outside the interval, above its upper bound" — lets a reader interpret the numbers without reconstructing the scheme from the body. A caption that reads only "Results for the four problems" forces the reader back into the text and defeats the purpose of the exhibit as a standalone unit.

## No Exhibit Introduces a Term First

An exhibit may not be the first or sole place a term, metric, or named entity appears. Because exhibits are read out of sequence, a name that lives only in a figure or table has no anchor in the argument — the reader meets it cold, with no gloss. Every named study, metric, and term in an exhibit must already appear and be glossed in the running prose. Audit each exhibit for orphan terminology: a row, label, or column heading naming something the prose never mentions is the signal. This is the exhibit-side reciprocal of the no-orphan-terms rule in <ctxmod_path path="ctx:github.com/ctxmod/writing_academic/context::shared::language@scholarly_prose/reference/terminology.md" />.

## Decode Figure-Internal Jargon

Shorthand inside a figure — abbreviations, formal terms, reserved labels — must be relabeled or glossed in plain words, in the figure or its caption. The reader of a figure has even less context than the reader of prose.

| In-figure shorthand | Plain relabeling |
| ------------------- | ---------------- |
| "bijective" | "one-to-one" |
| "analytes" | "laboratory tests" |
| an acronym used once | spelled out, with the acronym in parentheses |
| "reserved range / custom 2.1B" | "custom codes (no standard equivalent)" |

Distinct visual encodings must themselves be decoded: if arrows of different styles mean different things, the caption says what each style means in words.

## The Exhibit Supports, It Does Not Replace

An exhibit illustrates or substantiates a point; it does not stand in for the sentence that states the point. The argument must be followable in prose alone, with the exhibit strengthening it. A body that says only "see Figure 3" where a claim belongs has offloaded its argument to a place the linear reader has not yet reached. State the point in the text; let the exhibit carry the evidence and the detail. This complements the worked-example guidance in <ctxmod_path path="ctx:github.com/ctxmod/writing_academic/context::shared::language@scholarly_prose/reference/concreteness.md" />.

## Anti-Patterns

| Anti-Pattern | Consequence | Alternative |
| ------------ | ----------- | ----------- |
| Caption restates the title | Exhibit is uninterpretable alone | Decode the visual; state the takeaway |
| Term first appears in an exhibit | Reader meets a name with no anchor | Introduce and gloss in prose first |
| Undecoded figure-internal jargon | Figure reader has no context | Relabel or gloss shorthand in plain words |
| Distinct encodings left unexplained | Reader guesses what styles mean | Caption says what each encoding means |
| Argument offloaded to "see Figure N" | Linear reader hits a claim-shaped gap | State the point in text; exhibit supports it |
